Our Research

The Problem

Humans encounter a plethora of microbes daily. Innate immune cells are at the first responders to fend off disease caused by pathogenic microbes but can also cause life-threatening hyperinflammatory dysregulation. While pathogen-associated molecular patterns and/or cytokines are prime inducers of inflammatory responses, cell intrinsic and extrinsic factors including cell identity, tissue environment and even diet influence innate responses. Another regulatory layer of innate immune responses are past inflammatory encounters that result in durable epigenetic memory. This innate immune memory impacts secondary inflammatory responses and thereby affects disease pathology. There is a significant gap in our understanding of how cells integrate a myriad of immunological and/or environmental cues to mount adequate responses and elicit memory. Mapping the underlying molecular networks governing this complex yet fundamental cellular ability is a major frontier in biomedical research, with the potential to reveal novel concepts of immune regulation that will inform therapeutic strategies.

The Approach

In the Lercher lab, we take a holistic approach to decipher the black box of inflammatory signal integration and resulting epigenetic memory. We employ tractable primary cell culture systems and complementary murine infection models to study real-world pathogen encounters. We utilize next generation sequencing, real-time imaging, genetic engineering and immune profiling to build a framework of complex inflammatory signal integration and cell-cell communication networks across tissues in health and disease. Our overarching goal is to uncover fundamental principles of immunity that underly disease development, progression and resolution to enable new therapeutic approaches and boost pandemic preparedness.

The Questions

  • + How do cells integrate inflammatory cues to mount adequate immune responses?
  • + What are the determinants of cell-type or tissue-specific immune responses?
  • + How do innate immune responses lead to durable epigenetic memory?
  • + Which cellular and cross-organ communication networks shape innate immunity and memory?
  • + Can we use synthetic immunology to build circuits that control complex immune responses?

Alexander Lercher

Group Leader

Alex is from Austria. He moved to Heidelberg University to start his own lab at the Center for Integrative Infectious Disease Research (CIID) following his postdoc in the lab of Charlie Rice at Rockefeller University in New York, working on respiratory viruses and inflammatory memory. Before that, he did his PhD at CeMM in Vienna in the lab of Andreas Bergthaler, where he studied how chronic viral hepatitis reprograms liver metabolism and influences antiviral immunity.
